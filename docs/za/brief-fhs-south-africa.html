Flight Hour Solution (Pty) Ltd: what the new site needs

This brief is based on the FHS South Africa corporate profile and a review of flighthoursolution.com, the related Swiss company's site. The South African company covers much more ground than the Swiss site: ten service lines, sixteen client types, and visitors ranging from lessors to pilgrimage groups. So the site's first job is to route each visitor to the right door quickly, and the second is to earn trust as a company registered in 2026.

1Settle these before design starts

Most of these come from gaps in the profile. Several would make the site look unreliable, or cause legal trouble, if it launched without them.

Decide

The domain, and how to describe the link to the Swiss company

The profile lists www.flighthoursolution.com, which is the related Swiss company's site. Two options work: a section of that site (/za), or a separate flighthoursolution.co.za. The recommendation is the .co.za site, because the South African services, legal entity and privacy law are all different. Put a country switch on both sites and a clear line such as “Part of the Flight Hour Solution group”. Confirm the exact relationship first (subsidiary, sister company or partner) so that wording is accurate. Email stays on the group domain (confirmed: Thupudi@flighthoursolution.com). A .co.za site with .com email is common and reinforces the group link. Register flighthoursolution.co.za either way, if it's still available.

Blocker

The address says “TBA”

Nobody will buy an aircraft, an engine or a part from a trader with no physical address or phone number. South African law also expects the registered name, registration number and physical address on business websites (Companies Act s32; ECTA s43 where anything is sold online).

Decide

What FHS holds itself vs. what partners hold

List only real approvals and accreditations: SACAA-related approvals, parts-distribution accreditation, B-BBEE level, CSD registration. The profile itself commits to “avoiding unsupported technical or commercial claims”, and the site copy has to meet the same standard.

Decide

Who the team is

The profile names only the MD. The Swiss company's engineers and their 40+ years of engine experience are the strongest trust asset the new site has. If they will actually work on South African engine jobs, show them on the team page, labelled as group colleagues. Add two to four named people based in South Africa as well.

Decide

Which engines, aircraft and regions

The profile never says which engine or aircraft types FHS covers, or where it operates (South Africa, SADC, all of Africa?). Visitors check both before they get in touch. The Swiss site lists CFM56, LEAP, V2500, PW4000, Honeywell 131-9 APUs, TPE331 and TFE731. Reuse that list only for engines the group will actually support for South African clients.

Decide

How to present Engine 360

It's still in development. Present it as early access with a waitlist, never as a live product. The profile already says “is being designed”, so keep that wording.

Why each menu is grouped this way

  • Engines stays first. The profile calls engine management its primary focus, and “Engine intelligence” is the positioning line.
  • Engine 360 isn't a top-level item until it launches. It gets a homepage band and a menu entry with an Early access badge. A waitlist form can ask for fleet size, engine types and role, which doubles as market research.
  • Parts is a single page. Buyers arrive with a part number, not a category. Sixteen product categories don't need sixteen pages.
  • ACMI is merged into Aircraft. It appears in both section 5 and section 8 of the profile, and it's one decision for the customer.
  • Aircraft management goes under Aircraft, because the same owners and investors are the audience.

4Trust is the main design problem

A company registered in 2026 that trades aircraft, engines and parts will be checked hard. Buyers in these markets are wary of unverified sellers and chains of brokers. The profile's strongest material is its verification process, and at the moment none of that is visible to a site visitor. Put it on the site.

Publish the “How we work” sequence

The profile's five steps are a real sequence, so numbering them is justified. Put them on the About page and a condensed version on the homepage:

1 DefineRequirement, specs, timeline
2 VerifyRecords, counterparties, status
3 StructureDeal, lease or maintenance plan
4 ExecuteInspections, payments, delivery
5 MonitorPerformance and exposure

Show the transaction process on the Aircraft and Parts pages

The profile's nine-step major-transaction process (LOI → proof of funds → documents → escrow → inspection → purchase agreement → release) tells serious buyers what to expect. It also turns away people who aren't ready to transact, before they take up the team's time. Show it as a simple timeline.

Add a “How we verify parts” section

Include traceability, release documentation, certification status, incident and accident history, and remaining life. The profile already lists all of these, and they are what an AOG buyer checks before paying.

Add the charter disclaimer

The profile already states that FHS isn't the operating airline and that flights are operated by licensed operators under their own Air Operator Certificates. Put this on every charter page and on every charter quote. It protects the company and it tells a government or corporate buyer that the booking is legitimate.

Add proof, as soon as there is some

  • Team bios, with prior employers (airline, MRO, OEM, lessor)
  • The group link. The Swiss company's engine experience and capabilities can go on the site, as long as they're clearly labelled as the group's.
  • Anonymized case examples after the first jobs. Until then, show the group's and the team's experience.
  • Company facts in the footer: registration number, address, and the MD's name

5One form per door

A single generic contact form fits nobody here. A charter request and a parts request need completely different fields. Each form should ask only for what the customer is willing to share at first contact.

Every form needs a POPIA consent checkbox and a link to the privacy notice. Route each form to the person who handles that line of business. Charter and parts requests go stale within hours.

Don't publish the MD's personal address as the only contact. Set up role addresses on the group domain, for example charter@, parts@ and aog@, and forward them to whoever handles each door. People can then be added or replaced without changing the site. The Swiss company already uses contact@flighthoursolution.com, so agree the new addresses with whoever runs the group's email.

6South African specifics

  • Build mobile-first and keep pages light. Most South African traffic is on phones and data is expensive. Compress images and skip autoplay video in the hero.
  • Make WhatsApp the urgent channel. It's the default for business across the region. Use pre-filled messages for AOG parts and charter quotes, so the first message already contains the part number or route.
  • Comply with POPIA. This means a privacy notice, a consent checkbox, and an Information Officer registered with the Information Regulator. Check whether a PAIA manual is required, and link it in the footer if so.
  • Show B-BBEE and CSD for government buyers. The profile lists government institutions and state-owned airlines as clients. Their procurement teams look for a B-BBEE level (certificate or EME affidavit) and a CSD supplier number. Show both if FHS holds them.
  • State the currency. Aircraft, engines and parts trade in USD. Charter may be quoted in ZAR. Say which on each form.
  • Give availability in SAST. For example, “AOG replies within 2 hours, 07:00–22:00 SAST”. Only promise what the team can actually staff.
  • Consider French and Portuguese later. Mining and humanitarian charter work in the DRC, Mozambique and Angola may justify a French and Portuguese Charter section. English is enough at launch.

7Turning the profile into web copy

  • Cut the long lists. The profile has about 170 bullet points, which suits a tender document but not a web page. Each web page should lead with the problem and the outcome, then show four to six grouped capabilities. The full list can go in an expandable “Full scope” section for procurement readers.
  • Use one brand across both companies. The same logo, colours and “Keeps you flying” tagline should appear on both sites, so a visitor who switches country knows it's the same group. “Engine intelligence. Operational certainty.” can be the South African hero line, or the whole group can adopt it.
  • Add a plain subline under the hero. The positioning line alone doesn't say what FHS does. Something like: “Engine management, aircraft and parts sourcing, and charter for airlines, lessors and operators across [region].”
  • Keep the careful wording. Phrases like “through appropriately approved MROs” and “subject to aircraft availability” are there on purpose. Don't let copywriting turn them into promises.
